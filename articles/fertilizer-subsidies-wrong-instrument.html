The Wrong Instrument

Why Fertilizer Subsidies Have Not Closed Kenya's Maize Yield Gap

By Vasilis Ektoras 14 Oct 2026 19 min read

Abstract

Since 2008, the government of Kenya has been providing some kind of subsidy for fertilizers, spending KSh 31 billion in total on NCPB alone up until 2018, while the introduction of National Fertiliser Subsidy Programme (NFSP) in 2022 ensured that all registered farmers receive it according to their size of landholdings. It is a common knowledge that fertilizer users produce more than non-users, while subsidy receivers produce more than subsidy non-receivers, with a gap of at least 30%. The purpose of this paper is to prove that these figures, though accurate, serve as an excuse for introducing a policy instrument which fails to solve the problem at hand. There are four problems with the universal price subsidy program in particular that we have identified in the context of Kenya. First, price is rarely a constraint: in a famous study of Kenya's maize farmers' adoption (Duflo, Kremer and Robinson), the annual average returns from top-dressing fertilizers amount to 70%, with 24-43% adoption rate, while the small free-delivery program introduced right after harvest increased it by 14-18%, while 50% price subsidy did the same thing by 13 points. The constraint is the failure to shift the revenue from the harvest to plant; it is not the cost at planting. Secondly, the agronomic benefits are highly heterogeneous: According to Marenya and Barrett, in Western Kenya, it is not economical for fertilizer to be used in about a third of all the plots due to low organic matter in the soil, with poorer people working on the low-productive plots, making the subsidy less pro-poor than expected. Thirdly, majority of the payments made would be inframarginal as 70% of the usage was already recorded even before the introduction of the subsidy policy; 85% of those who were benefiting from the previous targeted fertilizer subsidy program were already buying the product regularly, and the effect on sales is that each kilogram of subsidized fertilizer replaced just 0.2 kg of commercial fertilizer sales. Fourthly, the risk of poor quality fertilizers arising from the government channel of distribution was experienced in the 2024 scandal involving fertilizers of poor quality.

1. The Question

Maize is the staple food in Kenya and smallholder farmers are its leading producers. The issue that raises the need to conduct this paper is the gap between yields and their persistence. According to a recent study conducted in counties, the average maize yield was around 2.2 tonnes/ha against an agronomic potential of 5.5 tonnes/ha while the usage of fertilizer was still below the recommended level of 200 kg/ha of NPK and 150 kg/ha of CAN fertilizer per county.

The conventional explanation of the problem is that the prices of fertilizers are too high, or that farmers do not have enough money to purchase fertilizer before planting. The conventional solution that follows is straightforward - reduce prices. In Kenya, this solution has been implemented on a big scale and for a long period of time, starting from the National Accelerated Agricultural Inputs Access Program (NAAIAP) through the NCPB program that started in 2008 until today through the NFSP program in 2022.

Previous studies in this research stream have consistently found that development challenges in Kenya identified as arising from "access issues" actually arise from instrument mismatch, where the solution increased financial access without impacting the compliance costs that dictate formalization, or where the influx of venture capital in Kenya did not reach the median youth-owned firm since the instrument was geared towards another type of enterprise. This paper poses the question as to whether subsidy programs for fertilizers fit this pattern as well.

The question is therefore not "does fertilizer raise yields?" — it plainly does — but: is the price of fertilizer at planting time the constraint that actually binds for the farmers Kenya is trying to reach, and if not, what is a universal price subsidy actually buying?

2. Four Mechanisms, Only One of Which a Price Subsidy Addresses

Price subsidy is a precise measure. With price subsidy, the price of each unit of fertilizer at the point of sale is reduced. Thus it will work if and only if the factor behind the low level of fertilizer use by the farmers is the fact that the price at point of sale is relatively higher than its expected benefits. There are four main reasons for fertilizer under-adoption in Kenya.

Mechanism 1: Intertemporal mismatch and present bias. The income of the smallholder farmer is irregular. The money comes at harvest time, when the need for it arises many months later, during planting time. The farmer, who at the time of harvesting was intending to buy fertilizer, could realize at the time of planting that he had already used up the cash in educating himself or his family, treating illness, social responsibilities, or even on consumption. Technically speaking, this is an issue of savings and commitment and not prices. In their paper, Duflo, Kremer, and Robinson have described it as stochastic presence bias and partial naivety – patient enough at the time of harvesting to plan for investment but impatient at the time of decision making, and lacking knowledge about their own future impatience to make plans. In such a scenario, a reduction in the price of the product at the time of planting becomes irrelevant as the problem is not that of the high cost of the fertilizer but lack of money at planting time.

Mechanism 2: Heterogeneous agronomic returns and soil quality. Nevertheless, the concept of a stable return per unit fertilizer application is scientifically wrong in the agronomic sense. As shown in the study by Marenya and Barrett, maize responds to nitrogen in the same way as in the von Liebig’s response to soil organic matter, there is a certain level of soil organic matter, beyond which additional nitrogen will not increase the output since nitrogen is no longer a constraining factor. Moreover, soil acidity makes the situation more problematic, because when pH is less than 5.0, aluminum toxicity affects the root system and prevents nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium uptake by plants, making fertilizers partially ineffective despite their cost. There are significant variations in return per unit fertilizer among various fields, but still there is only one price for all fields.

Mechanism 3: Inframarginality and market displacement. The subsidy will only affect those farmers who would not have bought the input even if the subsidy were not there. The farmers who buy the inputs in the market (inframarginals) will get income transfer purely. It may be argued that this is welfare economics, but nothing additional is being produced due to this. Worse still, the government subsidized fertilizer is competing with fertilizers of agro-dealers. So some part of government subsidized fertilizer is actually substitution of commercial fertilizers rather than addition to it.

Mechanism 4: Delivery-system and quality risk. Every subsidy that involves passing the physical inputs via the state distribution monopoly will carry the same risks as those associated with that channel. If a farmer gets poor-quality or adulterated fertilizer, they will be affected more than the farmer who got nothing; the former would have spent money, foregone the other option, and used the product which might end up destroying their crops for that season. This is a problem that a monetary payment or voucher system doesn't have.

Only Mechanism 3's income-transfer component and the narrow price channel are addressed by a price subsidy. Mechanisms 1, 2 and 4 are untouched by it, and Mechanism 4 is actively created by the delivery design.

3. Evidence

3.1 The yield associations are real but are not causal estimates of subsidy effect

In a study conducted on 300 maize farmers in Malava and Mumias East sub-counties of Kakamega County, it was found out that subsidy group farmers used 85.6kg/ha and produced 2.46t/ha while the non-participants group used 74.9kg/ha and produced 1.97t/ha. After controlling for fertilizer use, seed quality and household characteristics, there was an increase in maize productivity among the participating farmers by 32.3%. Subsidy fertilizer constitutes approximately 59 percent of the total inorganic fertilizer used by participating farmers.

Evaluating NAAIAP (Mason et al., 2017 as cited by UC Davis BASIS Program) indicates that there is an increase in productivity by 30 percent and in poverty severity by 11 percent. Tegemeo Institute reports that fertilizer farmers are 50 percent more productive than non-fertilizer farmers. These are indeed true observations. The main point here is to know what is being measured in the experiments. For the Kakamega experiment, the observations are cross-sectional between those who are participating and those who are not. Participation is not randomized and hence those who participated could be different in terms of information availability, proximity to depots and quality of land. On the other hand, for the Tegemeo experiment, the observations are on fertilizer users and non-users as opposed to subsidized and unsubsidized farmers respectively. Both do not control for the isolated effect of the subsidy.

3.2 The experimental evidence says timing beats price

Among the most rigorous studies that investigate Kenyan farmers growing maize, Duflo, Kremer and Robinson in Western Province stand out. Specifically, the authors examine 1,230 smallholders in this area during 2000-2004. There are three major results of their research relevant to this paper.

First, returns were high, but adoption was low. Top-dressing brought an approximate 70% annualized return in Western Province. At the same time, only 43% of farmers interviewed in Busia district ever used fertilizer, and among those included in the research, fertilizers use was 24% in the first season and 26% in the second one. It seems difficult to explain this result using a price constraint theory since the product was profitable even at existing prices, while people preferred not to purchase it.

Second, a small timely offer compensated a large untimely offer. The Savings and Fertilizer Initiative (SAFI) proposed free delivery of fertilizers upon purchasing right after harvesting period. Basic SAFI increased fertilizer use by 14 percentage points in the first season and by 18 points in the second season – 69% above the baseline level. The other type of offer allowing choosing delivery date ahead increased usage by 22 points.

On the other hand, the 50% subsidy on the price which took place two to four months after harvest raised usage levels by 13 percentage points. A free delivery nudge supplied at the right time proved equally effective, and sometimes even more effective, compared to a 50% price subsidy supplied at the wrong time.

Thirdly, what made the difference in this experiment was the timing and not the inducement. Providing a free delivery nudge a little bit later, instead of right after harvest, had no effect on the usage rate. The same inducement but offered a few months later became ineffective, which is difficult to explain using any price theory or transaction costs. Furthermore, the fact that enrolment in the SAFI program did not increase usage in later periods makes learning theory of motivation impossible. Farmers were not learning about how to use fertilizer; they were helped in implementation of their plan.

The calibration of the authors showed that small and limited-time discounts generate higher welfare levels compared to laissez-faire and subsidies because they help people who are biased towards present make a commitment while not forcing others to overuse.

3.3 Soil quality caps the return, and does so regressively

According to Marenya and Barrett, using plot-level data for western Kenya, fertilizer investments on average are profitable, but for about a third of the plots, soil organic matter depletion brings the marginal product of nitrogen down to a level that makes the profitability lower than prices would imply. In addition, they observe that poorer farmers farm the plots characterized by soil organic matter depletion, implying that fertilizer-based policies can actually be less pro-poor than usually thought and even widen inequalities.

This should get more attention in Kenyan policy discussion. The implication here is that the universal subsidy is distributionally inequitable because two farmers receive the same subsidy but the richer farmer gains more because he gets extra grain production from the subsidy.

The acidity of the soil also shows a tendency towards similar results. 13% of the total soil of Kenya is acidic soil. In such soil, the effectiveness of the fertilizer becomes low due to the toxicity of aluminum, resulting in disruption in the functioning of roots. The following data is presented from the experiments carried out by KALRO and IFDC in Western Kenya in order to explain the extent to which the output may respond to non-fertilizer methods: the output of the maize crop without using any input with an output rate of 0.53 t/ha became 1.59 t/ha upon using 2 t/ha of lime, and it became 2.84 t/ha upon the use of 4 t/ha of lime, with the lime and phosphorus having the same effectiveness as phosphorus alone in this experiment. (Note: The source data table titles have inconsistencies in itself; therefore, the data needs to be verified from the source.)

3.4 Most of the money is an inframarginal transfer

Furthermore, the evidence of displacement represents another element which raises questions regarding the universality of the design of the programme. The programme was launched when adoption rates already had reached up to 70% in the country via marketing channels because of the 27% reduction in the prices of fertilizers, which occurred due to the liberalization of the input market in the 1990s. According to the Tegemeo survey, 85% of respondents who benefitted from the NAAIAP programme bought fertilizers in 2000, 2004, and 2007. In accordance with Makau et al., every kg of subsidized fertilizer from NCPB was accompanied by the displacement of 0.2 kg of commercial fertilizer. Moreover, according to World Bank, the delay in the compensation from the government led to lower confidence of agrodealers to restock the subsidized fertilizer.

In contrast, the NAAIAP programme was able to cover 537,218 farmers by 2016 with the cost of KSh 517 million, while the cost of the NCPB programme reached KSh 31 billion by 2018.

3.5 Thin margins and the risk problem

In the analysis of their review, Jayne et al. find the value-cost ratio of fertilizer in Kenya to be between 1.05 (Western and Central Kenya) and 3.7 (lowland Eastern areas), and note that a value-cost ratio of less than 2.0, even though it is profitable on the aggregate scale, typically cannot compensate for the risk of crop failure due to drought or flooding. The farmer who refuses to purchase some costly input with the value-cost ratio of 1.05, having an idea of the probability of crop failure, does not behave rationally.

This is the uneasy combination. The relationship between productivity calculated in the Kakamega study is 32.3%. According to Jayne et al., western Kenya lies at the very edge of the range of value-cost ratios on the national level. Both may be right – high proportional increase in productivity with the low base and high price of the input which uses up most of the value. This means that exactly in the place where the increase in productivity was discovered, there may be no income increase.

3.6 The delivery channel failed in 2024

The Kenya Bureau of Standards in March 2024 established that the fertilizer supplied by KEL Chemicals had failed the necessary test parameters, including distribution of consignments between 5th and 10th March 2024 and brands such as Kelphos Plus, Kelphos Gold, and NPK 10:26:10. KEBS stated that there was a withdrawal of 5,840 bags, out of which 560 bags were seized from the NCPB depot in Molo, Nakuru County on 23rd March 2024. The government suspended the operations of KEL Chemicals and deferred the distribution of NCPB, asking farmers to avoid the use of the fertilizer. Media reports have not provided the number of farmers who had used the fertilizer before its withdrawal.

However, what is economically important here is not the scandal but the manner in which it has been conducted. Distribution of the input supply program via only one state distribution agency puts all quality risks in one place. The farmer using poor quality fertilizer has lost the cost of the input and his potential yield in the current farming season and, most importantly, behavioral design trust for the institution in the next season.

4. Argument

Read together, the evidence supports a conclusion stronger than "subsidies are poorly targeted." It is that the universal price subsidy is the wrong instrument for three of the four constraints that actually limit Kenyan maize yields, and is the direct cause of the fourth.

It is the experimental results that swing the balance. Given the fact that the fertilizer was already yielding a yearly profit rate of about 70% in the Western region with adoption levels being about 24% to 43%, it becomes clear that much of the reasons behind non-adoption were not related to high cost relative to return on investment, but rather to the lack of money at the right moment. The important conclusion from that experiment is the difference between the effect of offering free delivery upon harvest (+14 to +18 points, or +22 if done earlier) and the reduction of the price by 50% done much later (+13 points). The reduction in price is an expensive way out of the situation, while the offer of free delivery is quite cheap. The success of the latter method helps determine how many of the problems were connected to timing, not to the price.

The soil analysis reveals why the price channel is performing poorly in the few scenarios that it does work. For example, if fertilization is not economical on one-third of the land due to low soil organic content and acidic soils comprising around 13% of the land in Kenya limit nutrient uptake unless lime is applied, then subsidizing nitrogen fertilizers will encourage usage of the fertilizer in regions that do not have the ability to absorb the input. The sad truth is that those regions that cannot utilize the fertilizer are owned by poor farmers and, therefore, the impact of the program will be skewed towards wealthier farmers.

The inframarginality analysis completes the cost story. Given that 70% of fertilizer is being used before subsidies and 85% of those who previously participated in the programs had a consistent demand for fertilizer, the marginal cost of producing additional kilograms of fertilizer per shilling distributed is much smaller than the distribution quantities suggest.

Finally, the mechanism of delivery turns an inefficiency in finance into an operational risk. The 2024 event is the risk tail of the problem of delivering the national input through a single delivery mechanism.

This makes sure that the paper fits squarely within the topic of the series. For the M-Pesa paper, the extent of financial inclusion had no impact on the cost of regulation. For the youth entrepreneurship paper, there was lots of capital around, but was badly allocated relative to the average firm. In this case, the fertilizer subsidies have been extensive, but not much attention has been paid to the real constraints: inability to move money from the time of harvesting to the time of planting; agronomic constraints due to poor, acid soil; and the marginality of the business once cost of risk is considered.

5. A Testable Alternative: Harvest-Time Commitment Vouchers

The most effective contribution that can be made through these writings is to suggest an alternative way of doing things rather than pointing out the mistakes of the existing one. The above-mentioned evidence makes it clear as to what the alternative should be.

According to the conclusion of Duflo et al., the binding constraint in this case is the inability of farmers to move their money from harvest to planting season, and further, as per the conclusion, commitment device is effective in dealing with the problem in the harvest stage. On the other hand, in one of the writings included in this collection, it is seen that chamas exist in Kenya along with widespread mobile money in Kenya due to the fact that chamas provide commitment technology against present bias.

The mechanism described here is an input commitment product at harvest: the commitment is made by the farmer during harvest and involves setting aside a certain amount of money in a locked mobile-money sub-account redeemable as an input voucher from a registered agro-dealer during a designated planting period, with the subsidy coming through as an addition to the committed amount and not a discount on the transaction. We obtain four design criteria directly from the data. First, the commitment occurs at harvest time when the intention and cash are present. The lock substitutes for the social enforcement brought about by the chama system. Redemption by private competing agro-dealers maintains the agro-dealer network, and avoids the problem of the single-channel quality which came up in 2024. Furthermore, since the benefit will only accrue to those who make the commitment, the program targets itself at the marginal farmer. Inframarginal transfer costs will be minimized for those purchasing the inputs annually.

The testable comparison against the current design of the product will be the cost per additional kilogram of fertilizers per unit of responsive soil and not cost per bag.

6. Implications

  • For programme design: The case for shifting from a universal planting-time price discount toward a harvest-time commitment mechanism rests on experimental evidence, not on theory alone. At minimum, the two should be trialled against each other before the NFSP's structure is further entrenched.
  • For targeting: The combination of ~70% pre-existing adoption and 85% inframarginality among earlier recipients implies that self-targeting mechanisms are likely to outperform administrative targeting, which has historically failed to exclude existing buyers.
  • For agronomic complements: Soil testing and lime deserve policy attention proportionate to their measured effect. On acid soils, liming moved trial yields by margins comparable to fertilizer application. A fertilizer subsidy without a soil-amendment programme subsidizes an input onto land that cannot use it.
  • For market structure: Voucher-based redemption through competing agrodealers preserves the private distribution network that delivered 70% adoption before subsidies existed, and distributes quality risk rather than concentrating it.
  • For measurement: Programme evaluation should report cost per additional tonne of maize, not farmers reached or bags distributed. The former is the policy-relevant quantity; the latter two are insensitive to the inframarginality and soil-heterogeneity problems documented here.

7. Original Research Proposals

Three projects follow directly from the gaps identified above, in ascending order of difficulty.

(a) Measuring the inframarginal share under the NFSP. A short structured survey asking NFSP recipients whether, and in what quantity, they purchased fertilizer in seasons before the programme, and whether they would have purchased this season absent the subsidy. This directly estimates the single number the current literature leaves least precisely pinned for the current programme. Sample requirements are modest and the instrument is short.

(b) Joint mapping of subsidy uptake and soil response. Pairing programme uptake records with soil test results (organic matter and pH) at plot level in one or two counties would test whether subsidized fertilizer is disproportionately reaching plots in the unresponsive tail that Marenya and Barrett identify. If it is, the policy implication — pair the subsidy with soil amendment, or condition it on soil testing — is immediate and actionable.

(c) A small harvest-commitment pilot. The design in Section 5 could be piloted at small scale against a conventional subsidy arm, measuring take-up, redemption, applied quantity and yield.

This replicates the Duflo–Kremer–Robinson logic in the contemporary mobile-money environment, which did not exist when the original experiments ran. Given that the original study used physical delivery vouchers in the early 2000s, testing whether the same mechanism works through M-Pesa rails is a genuine and publishable contribution rather than a replication.

8. Limitations

The Duflo–Kremer–Robinson results, while experimentally identified, come from Western Province between 2000 and 2004 and from a sample of 1,230 farmers; Kenya's input markets, mobile money infrastructure and subsidy architecture have all changed substantially since. Their external validity to the 2026 NFSP context is an assumption of this paper, not a demonstrated fact, and it is the single assumption on which the central argument most depends.

Several supporting figures are drawn from a secondary research summary (UC Davis BASIS) reporting results from studies this paper did not read in the original, including Mason et al. (2017), Sheahan et al. (2014), Makau et al. (2016), Ariga et al. (2009) and Jayne et al. (2018). These should be verified against the primary sources before external citation.

The Kakamega study's authors and publication year were not visible on the repository page consulted. It is a cross-sectional comparison of 300 farmers in two sub-counties and is vulnerable to selection on unobservables; its 32.3% figure is an association, not a causal estimate.

The lime trial figures come from a presentation whose own table headings the source flags as unclear, and the benefit-cost ratios it reports (95.02 and 24.51) are implausibly large on their face and almost certainly reflect a units or labelling issue. They are cited here only for the direction of the yield effect, not the magnitude of the returns.

The juxtaposition in Section 3.5 — a large proportional yield gain in a low-profitability region implying small net income gains — combines results from separate studies with different samples and methods. It is our inference and has not been tested directly.

Most of the quantitative evidence concerns NAAIAP and the NCPB scheme rather than the 2022 NFSP, whose effects were not reported in the sources reviewed. The 2024 KEL Chemicals episode is documented through press reporting of KEBS findings; the number of farmers who applied affected product is not established.

Finally, this paper does not produce a cost-per-additional-tonne estimate for any Kenyan programme, which is the figure that would most cleanly adjudicate between the instruments it compares.
