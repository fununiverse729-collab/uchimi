Youth Entrepreneurship and Capital Constraints

Why Kenyan Startups Struggle to Scale Past the Micro Stage

By Vasilis Ektoras Mitropoulos 10 Jul 2026 9 min read East Africa Series

The contradiction inherent in Kenya’s startup ecosystem is thus: Kenya has seen a historic influx of USD 984 million worth of venture capital in a particular year, and yet an estimated 60% of youth-led MSMEs fail within the first three years, and 68% of SMEs in Kenya continue to fund themselves using only retained earnings. In the view of this paper, the seeming paradox disappears once “capital” is disaggregated into its forms: Kenya is not in need of capital per se, but rather of the specific form of capital: growth finance in the form of revenue-based debt, or small tickets of non-collateralized finance. The available formal forms of capital are geared towards either the form of capital requiring collateral or the form of venture style capital that requires significant growth. The outcome is the continued dependence of normal youth-led businesses on self-financing which necessarily restricts their growth potential to the cashflow of the entrepreneur himself and thus turns the “micro” stage from a transition point into a natural limitation.

1. The Question

The youth entrepreneurship system in Kenya has its share of momentum: thanks to mobile money, delivery services, and reduced costs for digital services, it is easier for people to become entrepreneurs now than ten years back, and there are government initiatives like Digital Entrepreneurship Accelerator Program and the newly announced Nyota youth fund that encourage youth to become entrepreneurs. One of the most consistent characteristics of the Kenyan private sector is the phenomenon of the “missing middle,” i.e., inability of SMEs to progress beyond the micro-business level, and the “missing middle” applies predominantly to youth-owned firms.

This paper asks a specific version of the missing-middle question: given that Kenya is simultaneously attracting record levels of investment capital and watching the majority of youth-led MSMEs fail or stagnate at micro scale, where exactly does the capital that exists fail to reach the firms that need it, and why?

2. The Microeconomic Mechanism

What explains the “missing middle”? The missing middle refers to a particular funding problem that is faced by SMEs (small to medium size firms; typically in Kenya, firms which employ 5-250 employees and have capital requirements of about USD 20,000-2,000,000). The conventional answer to the missing middle is simply: “lack of access to credit”. I posit that the more precise mechanism is a tool mismatch in terms of the pecking order theory of corporate finance, according to which firms generally seek to finance expansion out of internal sources first, then debt, and then equity, as subsequent sources involve higher information and control costs. With a properly functioning financial system, a business will use up low-cost internal sources and seamlessly graduate up the ladder. In the case of entrepreneurship among youth in Kenya, however, each of the latter two steps of the ladder is individually broken, for reasons that:

The debt rung is broken by collateral and recovery costs. For example, in Kenyan commercial banks, the interest rates at which the lending to SMEs takes place is around 18%, with recovery expenses due to defaulted debt of 40% of the borrowed money. This situation is directly linked with the inefficiency in enforcement of collateralization (as pointed out in the land titling paper of this series), as well as the contract enforcement in general. It is only natural for banks to react to such a risky environment by asking for collateral that most new firms lack.

The equity rung is broken by a structural mismatch in what investors want versus what most local firms can offer. Equity investment in its formal incarnation, venture capital and angel investment, requires the degree of sophistication, exit planning, and fast-growing path (usually 10%-30% per annum) that equity investment is expected to have in the developed markets. Most of the youth-owned Kenyan MSMEs - shops, textile firms, food services, small-scale traders - do not provide these things, not due to any lack of desire on their part but rather due to their business models being of such nature that they cannot provide what this instrument requires of them.

With both rungs broken, firms are pushed back down to the bottom of the ladder, self-financing, and stay there. This is the essence of the missing middle, it is not the fact that Kenyan youth entrepreneurs do not look for capital; rather, it is the fact that the two most common formal sources of capital are tailored for businesses different from the typical small local firm, and the sole choice left is retained earnings, which limits growth to internal growth rates.

3. Evidence

Self-financing dominance is the clearest quantitative signature of the mismatch. An estimated 68 percent of SMEs in Kenya fund their operations by retained earnings. This clearly proves that the debt and equity steps as stated above are not accessible to most of the firms, which is in line with the predictions of the pecking order in case the higher steps are unavailable instead of being costly.

The debt rung’s specific breakage is quantifiable. The cost of lending to Kenyan SMEs is about 18% in interest rates and 40% in the cost of debt collection, which makes the bank either ration credit to those who can secure their lending by collateral or lend at such exorbitant interest rates that the moderate risk-moderate growth young enterprises are priced out of the market.

The equity rung’s breakage is visible in where the record capital inflows actually go. Despite Kenya’s USD 984 million in venture funding in a recent reporting year and 52% Annual growth in investment notwithstanding, Village Capital’s detailed report on East African startup funding shows that 87 percent of investments disclosed in East Africa were into companies with at least one European or North American founder, revealing that the existing formal equity capital, despite its abundance, is being deployed in an environment and according to criteria that disproportionately benefit a small segment of the ecosystem, and not the average young entrepreneur. In addition, their reporting shows growth in funding skewed heavily towards debt funding aimed at companies dealing with energy and climate technology, which is far removed from the usual capital-light retail business carried out by the majority of youth entrepreneurs.

Failure rates confirm the capital gap is binding, not just inconvenient. It is estimated that up to 60% of MSMEs led by young people fail in the first three years, which is noticeably higher than the 50% estimated failure rate within five years commonly reported in the case of SMEs in developed economies like the United Kingdom, considering that failure among businesses in Kenya takes place against a backdrop where there is much weaker safety netting for any founder who fails (predatory debt recovery system as opposed to bankruptcy protection). The failure rates expected by the Kenyan government itself through its Nyota youth fund (a KES 5.28 billion capital grant program to benefit 120,000 young people) is 20%, yet according to industry experts (like Seacom East Africa), the real failure rates are much higher at about 60%.

Qualitative evidence points to talent and networks compounding the pure capital problem. Research on youth-led SMEs (one example being the stage model based research by Churchill & Lewis on garment businesses in the Nairobi CBD) has highlighted capital challenges together with the issue of human capital, which complements the capital challenge since Kenya is placed 23rd out of 29 countries for indicators of entrepreneurship linked to tertiary education, and the business firms point to lack of managerial capacity as contributing to the limitations of growth. Successful case studies of youth micro-entrepreneurs have demonstrated how mobile money and delivery services have helped alleviate operational challenges; however, this addresses operational rather than growth challenges of capital.

4. Argument

The evidence clearly favors analyzing the “missing middle” in the context of youth entrepreneurship in Kenya through the lens of capital matching failure rather than capital shortage failure. There is simply too much total investment going into the Kenyan economy for such a straightforward explanation to be valid. The problem, therefore, lies in the lack of a mid-range financing vehicle that will be neither a large venture capital nor an institutional loan from a commercial bank but would provide adequate financing to the average youth enterprise, whose core business would have more to do with stable local trade than high growth and exit via IPO. Banks, as we know, finance only those businesses that meet certain selection criteria, namely collateral and established credit history. Venture capital funding targets businesses that possess both rapid growth potential and extensive network of connections, including international ones. That is why enterprises that fall between those two types of funding get marginalized within the financing hierarchy, thus explaining high self-funding ratio and failure rates among youth micro/small/medium enterprises.

It is also the reason behind the missing middle being particularly binding for young entrepreneurs: young entrepreneurs are more likely to lack collateral experience that will allow them to get debt from banks as well as the network effects that play a role, according to Village Capital, in accessing the equity financing available in the ecosystem.

5. Implications

  • For policy and development finance design: In this context, there is clear evidence that suggests the need to focus on financial products that directly address the gap noted above, such as revenue finance or cash-flow finance based on digital transaction data (similar to mobile money-based underwriting discussed earlier in this series within the context of the land title paper), as opposed to simply raising the supply of bank finance and/or venture capital, which has proven to be systematically exclusionary of this group of firms.
  • For youth-focused programs like Nyota: Since even governmental programs seem to be underestimating actual attrition rates, combining capital grants with human capital assistance (training of mid-level management, mentorship programs), which research indicates, multiplies the pure financing problem, could enhance survival rates better than capital grants alone.
  • For future primary research: The logical next step in this research project would be a survey of young entrepreneurs, preferably including both those who received formal financing and those who did not, on exactly which pecking order rung they tried to reach and where their efforts failed, at the debt stage, equity stage, or neither, in order to see whether the model proposed above is corroborated by personal experience.

6. Limitations

Some of the numbers discussed above (the 60% youth-MSME failure rate, the actual attrition rate of the Nyota Fund, and the investments allocation of the Startup Ecosystem Report 2026) are taken from the industry reports and journalism articles rather than peer-reviewed scientific literature and can only serve as preliminary data that needs to be confirmed by primary sources such as KNBS and the Central Bank of Kenya publications. Pecking Order Theory, although being a widely-accepted corporate finance framework, is discussed above at a relatively high level of aggregation; for testing, one would need the data on financing source order within the life cycle of a company. Lastly, in this analysis, "youth entrepreneurship" is considered a homogeneous concept for convenience, although there might be considerable differences in capital availability, availability of collateral, and network connectivity among sectors, genders, and regions.
